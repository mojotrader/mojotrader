What these numbers do not say

Everything above is a backtest read off two TradingView exports. Four things materially affect how the figures should be used.

Commission

The exports were not run on the same cost basis: Halyard's charged __RAW_COMM_A__ over the year, QuantFlow's charged __RAW_COMM_B__. Every figure on this page re-prices both at __COMM__ per contract per side (__COMM_RT__ round turn), which costs QuantFlow roughly a thousand dollars of its raw net profit. Slippage is still not modelled anywhere — at 765 round turns a single MNQ tick of slippage per side is about $8,000.

Capital base

The two testers ran different accounts — __CAP_A__ for Halyard against __CAP_B__ for QuantFlow — so the percentage returns are not like for like. Halyard's __ANN_A__ and QuantFlow's __ANN_B__ describe the same dollar engine measured against different denominators. Compare profit factor, expectancy and Sharpe across the two; compare the percentages only against their own capital.

Tail risk

Halyard's worst single day was __WORST_DAY_A__, or __WORST_DAY_A_PCT__ of its stated capital, and it runs up to four contracts. A book that can lose a tenth of its account in a session is sized for the backtest's best case, not for a gap. The joined book's peak of __CONTRACTS__ concurrent contracts is the number to check margin against, not the average of 2.57.

Sample

One year, one instrument, one regime — a strongly trending Nasdaq. 765 trades is a healthy sample for trade-level statistics but only thirteen independent months for the equity curve, so the Sharpe, Calmar and drawdown figures carry far less certainty than their decimal places suggest. No walk-forward or out-of-sample split is present in these exports.
